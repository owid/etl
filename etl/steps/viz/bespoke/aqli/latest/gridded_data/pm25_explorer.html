Air pollution, grid cell by grid cell —

Annual average concentration of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) in the air, worldwide, on a 0.1° (~11 km) satellite-derived grid. These are small particles that are 2.5 micrometers or less in diameter and can cause health problems in humans exposed to high concentrations. Drag the timeline to move through 1998–2024, search for one of the 1,000 largest cities, and hover anywhere for that cell's value.

This is total PM2.5, including natural sources. Mineral dust and sea salt are not removed, so the brightest areas on this map are the Sahara, the Arabian peninsula, the Taklamakan and the Gobi — deserts, where almost nobody lives. OWID's published “Exposure to air pollution from human sources” chart uses a different measure that strips those out. Do not read this map as that one.
2024 is drawn at higher resolution than the other years. It comes from the fine 0.01° source, rendered at about 5 km; 1998–2023 come from the coarse 0.1° grid at about 11 km. The badge beside the timeline shows which you are looking at. The map getting sharper on 2024 is a change in resolution, not in the data — and the full 0.01° world cannot be shown here at all, since at 36,000 × 13,000 px no browser will draw it as a single image.
Land only, and coverage stops at 60°S and 70°N. Antarctica, Greenland's interior and the high Arctic are blank — that is the product's extent, not missing data. The grid is masked to land before anything is drawn or counted: SatPM also emits values over coastal and enclosed water (the North Sea, the Great Lakes, shelf seas), which are 16% of all its data cells and would otherwise haze the coastlines and dilute every figure below. Every statistic here is over land.

Data source: SatPM V6.GL.03 — Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group, Washington University in St. Louis. Total PM2.5 at 0.1°, derived from MODIS, MISR, SeaWiFS and VIIRS retrievals combined with the GEOS-Chem model and calibrated against ground monitors.
